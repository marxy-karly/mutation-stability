01Problem formulation

Protein engineers often ask whether a single amino-acid change will make a protein more or less stable. Testing that takes one experiment per mutation. A model that ranks candidate mutations first would save most of that work, but only if it works on proteins it was not trained on.

Question
Given a protein's sequence and one amino-acid substitution in it, how well can the change in folding stability (ΔΔG) be predicted when the protein comes from a family the model never saw?
One row
One single-substitution variant of one small protein domain ( to residues), for example W34D: the tryptophan at position 34 replaced by aspartate.
Target
ddG_ML, the variant's stability minus the unmutated protein's, in kcal/mol. Positive means the mutation stabilizes the protein. A regression task.
When the prediction is made
Before any experiment, when only the sequence and the proposed swap exist.
Available then
The swap, its position, the protein's sequence, and a predicted structure of the unmutated protein (AlphaFold), which needs no experiment. Every measurement column in the file is an output of the experiment and is not used.
Limits of the claim
Single substitutions with a measurable ΔΔG, on this assay. Nothing about multiple mutations, insertions or deletions, and nothing about structures of the mutated protein.

    Leakage checks

    • Target in disguise. deltaG, dG_ML and the protease columns are what ΔΔG is computed from. None is a feature.
    • Relatives across the split. Every protein appears hundreds of times, once per mutation, and related proteins share a cluster. Whole clusters are assigned to one split, and a check confirms no cluster is in two.
    • Fitted steps. One-hot encoding and scaling sit inside each model's pipeline, so they are fitted on training rows only.
    • One split, frozen. The split files were made once and every experiment uses the same ones.

    04Baselines and methods

    Three baselines set the bar. A model is useful only if it beats the mean. It shows that position and context matter only if it also beats a table of the average effect of each swap, which knows nothing about position.

      05Validation protocol

      Split
      . Train mutations ( clusters), validation (), test (). No cluster is in two splits.
      Selection
      Every model is trained on the training rows. The learned model with the lowest validation MAE is selected.
      Final test
      All models are scored on the test set once. The test set plays no part in selection.
      Uncertainty
      A 95% interval for every test score, from resampling whole test clusters times. Plus repeated grouped splits of the training and validation rows, without touching the test set.

      Metrics

      • MAE (headline and selection): the typical miss in kcal/mol.
      • RMSE: punishes large misses more.
      • R²: share of the variance explained; 0 means no better than the mean.
      • Spearman: does the model rank mutations correctly, overall and within each protein? That is what a screening step needs.

      Cross-validated MAE on training and validation rows (kcal/mol, lower is better), for the same methods under two ways of making folds.

      Folds keep clusters togetherRandom folds

      Why split by cluster and not by row?

      Mutations of one protein share its backbone, and related proteins share a cluster. With relatives on both sides, a model can recognise the family and the test stops measuring performance on new proteins. The chart above shows how big that effect is.

      Why a substitution table as a baseline?

      Much of what is known about mutations is which swap they are: anything to proline, or a large residue replaced by a small one, tends to hurt. A table of those averages is cheap. A model that cannot beat it has not used position or context.

      Why intervals?

      The test set has independent clusters, far fewer than its rows suggest. A different set of clusters would give a different score; the interval shows how different.
